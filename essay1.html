Black Wednesday and the collapse of the British Pound

Published on 09 March 2026

On Wednesday, 16 September 1992 – now called “Black Wednesday” – investors across the world started aggressively selling British Pounds. The currency started losing value, and the government was forced to intervene.

The Bank of England spent around £27bn in foreign reserves buying pounds in the open market. When that didn’t help, the government raised interest rates from 10% to 12%, and then promised a further increase to 15%. It didn’t help.

At the time, the British economy was in a recession. It needed lower interest rates to support growth. A high interest rate of 15% would put a lot of pressure on an already struggling economy, and investors knew this wasn’t feasible. The government looked desperate – which also led to speculations that the central reserves weren’t enough to back the pound anymore. This sparked fears of an incoming currency collapse and led to a doubling down on the selling.

By the end of the day, the government accepted defeat and let the pound float. They also withdrew from the ERM, which had required Britain to maintain the pound within a narrow band of ±6%. This was a major political embarrassment. The UK had joined in October 1990 after a major political drama – which also saw the resignation of Margaret Thatcher. The new government had argued for deeper European integration and had promised German-style economic stability and prosperity. In the days after the incident, the pound lost almost 15% of its value.

The European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) had been set up in 1979. Its purpose was deeper monetary integration and stabilization of currency exchange rates within the European nations. The German Deutschemark – as the most stable currency in Europe – served as the de facto anchor. All other countries effectively tied their monetary policies to Germany. If the Deutschemark rose or fell in value, all other currencies would have to follow.

This was done through the central banks. To increase the value of its currency, a central bank would either directly buy its own currencies in the open market, or increase interest rates which would incentivize people to buy and hold. Increased demand would prop up the prices. Similarly, if they wanted to decrease the value of their currency, they would either sell their currency or decrease interest rates. Increased supply would then lower the prices.

However, there are limits to these methods. Central bank reserves – while huge – are finite, and buying or selling enough currencies to influence the global financial markets cannot be done indefinitely. Adjusting interest rates also has limitations because it directly impacts the domestic economy. An inflationary economy is an over-heated economy. It needs to be cooled. Raising interest rates makes borrowing – and thus, spending – more expensive, resulting in economic activity slowing down. On the other hand, a recessionary economy needs to be heated up, and lowering interest rates makes borrowing easier, which speeds up economic activity.

The UK – which was in a recession – should have lowered its interest rates but didn’t, because their monetary policy was tied to Germany. While Germany had been an extremely stable economy for a long time, in 1989 the Berlin Wall fell reuniting East and West Germany. The government spent massive amounts of money to develop the economically weaker East. The scale of the spending was so huge that it triggered fears of inflation. Germans remembered the hyperinflation of the 1920s, and hence, responded quickly. The Bundesbank – Germany’s central bank – raised interest rates to cool down the inflation.

The deutschemark rose in value. And the UK made the pound follow. When the UK had joined the ERM, it had done so at an exchange rate of £1 = 2.95 DM. Since then, the British economy had weakened significantly due to the recession, and was stuck defending the old exchange rate. An overvalued currency hurt the domestic economy. Exports were expensive. Investors knew this was unsustainable. Not that the central bank would run out of money – the political will would break in the face of domestic discontent.

Germany and Britain were experiencing very different economic realities. Germany was facing inflation, hence they raised interest rates. The UK was supposed to lower interest rates to support their recessionary economy, but couldn’t because of the ERM membership.

They were forced to share the same monetary policy – but it could not last for long. The short positions started building. George Soros himself built a position of around $10bn and it made his fund $1bn in profits. While he was given the title of “the man who broke the bank of England,” the reality is that Soros’ position, while significant, was relatively small compared to the market turnover. For context, the daily turnover of the global foreign exchange market in 1992 was about $1 trillion.

Ultimately, on September 16, 1992, the collapse came. The pressure had been building for months. Once enough people were convinced that the government couldn’t defend the peg for long, the end came quickly. The pound crashed, and the UK had to leave the ERM. Speculators made billions. But it wasn’t all doom and gloom for the British economy either. After the pound fell, exports became cheaper and more competitive, which helped economic growth. The GDP rose, unemployment fell. Exiting the ERM allowed the Bank of England to set interest rates according to domestic needs.

Black Wednesday wasn’t really a “disaster.” The speculators didn’t crash the pound. They accelerated it. But it allowed the failing British economy to revive itself, and highlighted the risks of rigid fixed-exchange systems for countries with different economic needs.
